SORT IT OUT

What you throw away doesn’t disappear.

It ends up in our soil, our water and our neighbors’ lungs. Sorting waste correctly and handling household and campus chemicals safely are two small habits that protect La Trinidad, Benguet, and everyone who lives, studies, farms or visits here.

Four bins, one habit

Philippine waste data group household waste into four types: biodegradable, recyclable, residual and special waste[1]. The Ecological Solid Waste Management Act (RA 9003) requires segregation at source[3]. Bin colors can differ by barangay, school or building, so always follow the labels where you are.

Green: biodegradable

Food scraps, fruit peels, leaves, paper towels, garden waste.

Can become compost.

Tip: keep a small covered bucket in the kitchen and empty it daily to avoid smell and flies.

Blue: recyclable

Clean paper, cardboard, plastic bottles, cans, glass.

Tip: do not flatten bottles. Empty them completely of liquids and other contents, then rinse and dry them so they can be recycled properly. Wet or greasy paper is not recyclable.

Rinse and dry first.

Black: residual

Candy wrappers, sanitary napkins, disposable diapers, plastic-lined cartons and broken ceramics[14].

The last resort bin.

Tip: shrink the pile first. Only about 18% of waste is truly residual[1], so before you toss, ask if it can be composted, recycled or avoided. Carry a reusable bag, bottle and food container, and choose refills over single-use sachets. Keep residuals dry in a sealed bag and put them out only on your barangay’s collection schedule.

Red: special and hazardous

Batteries, bulbs, old medicine, aerosol cans, e-waste, chemical containers.

Never mix with the others.

Tip: store it in a closed, labeled container away from children until you can bring it to a drop-off point.

Waste facts, with sources

The facts below come from the studies and official sources listed under “Sources”. The bracketed numbers point to them. National figures may differ from your barangay or campus, so check your local government’s waste data when you can.

What is in a Philippine waste bin?

DENR data from 2014, cited in the Philippine Senate, show the share of each waste type in municipal solid waste[1]. More than half is food and garden waste that could become compost. Tap a bar for examples.

Tap a bar to see what it includes.

The scale of the problem

A summary of the World Bank’s 2025 What a Waste database reports that the Philippines generates about 14.6 million tons of municipal solid waste each year, ranking 27th of 50 countries[2].

Waste and our rivers

A 2021 study in Science Advances estimated that more than 1,000 rivers account for 80% of the plastic that rivers carry to the ocean, and that small urban rivers are among the most polluting[4]. Its model ranked the Philippines highest, at about 36% of the global total[5].

This is a model estimate with substantial uncertainty, not a direct count, so treat the exact share with care[5].

Why food scraps matter for climate

In landfills, organic waste breaks down without oxygen and releases methane. Composting, which uses oxygen, releases carbon dioxide instead[7]. A study in Scientific Reports found composting food scraps produced 38 to 84% fewer greenhouse gas emissions than landfilling them[6].

What RA 9003 requires

Segregation at source (Sec. 21), a materials recovery facility (MRF) in every barangay or cluster (Sec. 32), and at least 25% of waste diverted from disposal through reuse, recycling and composting (Sec. 20)[3]. Open dumping and open burning of solid waste are prohibited[3].

Myth or fact?

“Food scraps are harmless in a landfill because they rot away.”

Myth. Buried food waste breaks down without oxygen and produces methane[7]. Keeping it out of the trash and composting it avoids much of that[6].

“Mixing cleaners makes them stronger.”

Myth, and a dangerous one. Bleach mixed with ammonia or with acids such as vinegar or toilet bowl cleaner releases toxic gases[9]. Use one product at a time, as the label directs.

“Burning our trash is a quick way to get rid of it.”

Myth. Open burning of solid waste is a prohibited act under RA 9003[3]. Segregate instead, and bring leftovers to your barangay MRF or collection.

In La Trinidad, Benguet

Our campaign supports what La Trinidad already asks of residents and visitors. These local facts come from news reports, a research article and government releases. Schedules and programs can change, so confirm the latest with the Municipal Environment and Natural Resources Office (MENRO) or your barangay.

Know the local rules

Municipal Ordinance 53-98, the Comprehensive Solid Waste Management ordinance, was enacted in February 2017[11]. MENRO posts collection schedules, do’s and don’ts and penalty reminders at collection points, and these are meant for visitors too[11]. People who dump garbage outside the schedule can be issued citation tickets[12].

Bantay Basura

Deputized volunteers at collection points check that waste is segregated at source and put out on schedule[13]. MENRO reported that the daily waste going to the landfill fell from about 50 to about 30 tons between July 2017 and February 2018 after the program began[13]. Funding for the program has been a concern[13].

Where residual waste goes

La Trinidad uses the Alno engineered sanitary landfill, whose space is limited, and the town has been reported hauling some waste to Capas, Tarlac[14]. Sorting well keeps more waste out of the landfill and makes it last longer[14].

Vegetable trimmings

La Trinidad has the largest vegetable trading post in the Philippines, with about 60 tons of vegetable trimmings a day reported in 2011[15]. Benguet State University’s trading center set up a rotary composting facility in 2017 to turn biodegradable trimmings into compost[16].

Pesticide containers

Benguet’s provincial ordinance calls for farmers to triple-rinse empty pesticide containers, with collection and disposal arranged by dealers and companies[17]. Hedcor reported collecting nearly 5,000 containers from La Trinidad, Bakun and Sablan from 2016 to 2018[18]. Never reuse these containers for food, water or drinks.

Why education matters

A Benguet State University study found that 53% of surveyed residents considered La Trinidad’s segregation scheme a success. Residents who rated barangay officials meeting and explaining the scheme as important were about 5.8 times more likely to call it a success[19]. This supports community education like ours.

The chemistry behind our campaign

This community immersion is a project for our Chemistry class. Every habit we promote rests on chemistry we study. Tap a card to see the reaction and how it connects to the campaign.

Acids, bases and redox: why bleach must never meet vinegar or ammonia
NaOCl + 2 HCl → NaCl + Cl₂ + H₂O
NaOCl + NH₃ → NH₂Cl + NaOH

Sodium hypochlorite in bleach is a strong oxidizer. With a strong acid it can release chlorine gas, and with ammonia it forms chloramine[9]. These equations are simplified, because the exact products depend on concentration and conditions.

Campaign link: our “never mix cleaning products” message.

Biochemistry: aerobic composting versus anaerobic landfills
Aerobic: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O
Anaerobic: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 3 CH₄ + 3 CO₂

With oxygen, microbes break food waste down mainly into carbon dioxide. Without oxygen, as in a landfill, they also release methane[7], a greenhouse gas reported to be up to 35 times as potent as carbon dioxide over a century[10]. Glucose stands in for food waste here, and real waste is a complex mix.

Campaign link: why we ask people to compost food scraps.

Neutralization: why labs sort waste by hazard type
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O + heat

Acids and bases react and release heat. Mixing incompatible wastes in one container can cause dangerous reactions, so laboratories use separate, labeled waste containers and follow each chemical’s safety data sheet.

Campaign link: the university lab rows in our chemicals table.

Conservation of mass: why burning does not make waste disappear

Atoms are conserved in chemical reactions. Burning rearranges them into smoke, gases and ash instead of removing them. Metals such as the mercury in fluorescent bulbs are elements, so they cannot be broken down and need special collection.

Campaign link: no open burning, and special waste goes to special collection.

Polymers: why we empty and rinse plastic bottles

Plastics are synthetic polymers, which are long chains of repeating units. Recycling sorts plastics by type, and leftover liquid or food in a bottle can contaminate that process, so we ask people to empty and rinse bottles first.

Campaign link: the blue bin tip.

Solutions and dilution: why pesticide containers are triple-rinsed
C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

Each rinse dilutes the residue left in a container, so three rinses leave far less than one. Benguet’s provincial ordinance calls for triple-rinsing empty pesticide containers[17]. This is a simplified idea, so always follow the product label.

Campaign link: pesticide containers on La Trinidad’s farms.

From waste to soil: our link to Agricultural and Biosystems Engineering

Compost made from food and garden waste is a soil amendment that can help reduce runoff and control erosion[7]. In La Trinidad, vegetable trimmings from the trading post are composted into fertilizer for farmers[15][16]. For ABE students, this connects chemistry, soil and waste management in one cycle.

Campaign link: green bin waste can return to the soil.

Chemicals at home and on campus

Everyday cleaners and lab supplies can hurt people, drains and rivers when they are mixed, poured away or thrown in the wrong bin. Hazardous waste is covered by RA 6969, and your school’s environmental health and safety office or your local government can tell you where to take it.

Never mix cleaning products. Bleach with ammonia makes toxic chloramine gas. Bleach with vinegar, toilet cleaner or other acids releases chlorine gas. Use one product at a time, with the window open.[9]

A 2020 CDC survey of 502 US adults found that only 35% knew bleach should not be mixed with vinegar, and 58% knew not to mix it with ammonia[8]. Share this page so more people know.
